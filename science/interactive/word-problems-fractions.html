Instructional script

Part 1: Launch and connection

Begin by inviting students to describe what they already know. Present a short scenario that naturally leads into the concept so the topic feels relevant before any formal rule is introduced.

Part 2: Core teaching

Introduce the symbols, vocabulary, definitions, and model. Highlight the pattern students should notice and explain the relationship between the numbers, quantities, or structures in the problem.

Part 3: Worked example

Use a complete worked example and narrate the decisions: what is known, what is unknown, what operation or relationship is appropriate, how to solve, and how to verify.

Part 4: Practice and reflection

Ask learners to try similar scenarios, compare strategies, and talk through mistakes without judgment. Reflection helps them build durable understanding rather than memorized shortcuts.

Teacher notes and misconceptions

Key misconception to watch: Students may treat the concept as a memorized procedure without understanding why it works. Emphasize the underlying structure, vocabulary, and real-world meaning at each step.

  • Ask students to explain the meaning of each symbol, step, and result in plain language.
  • Have students compare two methods and identify which is more efficient or more clearly connected to the concept.
  • Use multiple representations—words, diagrams, tables, equations, and examples—to support deeper understanding.
  • Pause during guided practice to ask, “What do we know?” “What are we solving for?” and “Does this answer make sense?”

Guided practice

Question 1.

Describe the concept of Word Problems Fractions using your own words and connect it to one real-life example.

Question 2.

Show one example that demonstrates the concept clearly and explain each step in complete sentences.

Question 3.

What is one common error students make with this topic, and how can they check whether their answer is reasonable?

Exit ticket

In 2–3 sentences, explain what you learned, what strategy you would use on a similar problem, and one thing you still need to review.

Optional extension

Students can create a short practice set or explain the concept to a classmate using a diagram, number example, or word problem. This extension deepens retention and helps students become more independent problem solvers.
